A release note tells you what changed. It rarely tells you what almost shipped, which assumption turned out to be wrong, or why a seemingly small feature took three weeks.

We want this blog to hold some of that missing context. Building in public, for us, means explaining choices while they are still specific enough to be useful.

## Show the work behind a decision

Last week, Alex wrote about [rebuilding our notification queue](/why-we-rebuilt-our-notification-queue/). The interesting part was not the diagram. It was the interrupted delivery that exposed a gap in our assumptions.

That is the kind of account we want to publish: a concrete problem, the options we considered, and the boundary of the solution we chose. Someone building a different product should be able to learn from it without adopting our entire approach.

## Leave room to change our minds

A public plan can start to feel like a promise. We will distinguish between work we have shipped, experiments we are trying, and ideas we have not committed to building.

If an experiment does not work, we want to say so. A confident launch announcement is easy to write. Explaining why we removed a feature is often more useful.

## Be open without being careless

Some details are not ours to share. We will not turn a private support conversation into a case study without permission, publish customer information, or treat sensitive operational details as material for an interesting post.

We can explain a tradeoff without exposing the people who helped us understand it.

## Make the record worth reading later

We are not committing to a daily stream of progress reports. We will write when there is something worth explaining, and link related decisions as the product develops.

Over time, we hope the result will be a clear account of how Good Company works—not just a sequence of things we announced.
