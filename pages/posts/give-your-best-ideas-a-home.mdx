Some things are easy to share once and surprisingly hard to find again.

A useful explanation disappears into a chat. A product decision lives in an old announcement. An essay gets a day of attention, then slips below everything that came after it.

A publication gives those ideas an address.

## Write for the second visit

The first visit might come from a link someone shared. The second often comes with a purpose: a reader wants to find that example, send a story to a colleague, or see what you've written since.

That changes how you think about a homepage. It can be a welcome, a reading list, and a map of what matters to you.

### Start with a few good pieces

You don't need a large archive. Three useful posts can tell a reader plenty:

- **An introduction:** who you are and what you'll write about.
- **An example:** something that shows your approach in practice.
- **A point of view:** an idea that gives someone a reason to come back.

That's the shape we're starting with on this blog.

## Connect the dots

Give each story a clear title. Link to an earlier piece when it adds context. If something changes, update the explanation people are still finding.

These are small editorial choices, but together they make a collection easier to explore. A reader can arrive through any post and discover another one worth their time.

> A useful archive grows one good page at a time.

## Find a rhythm you can keep

A publication doesn't have to move at the speed of a feed. You can write when there's something to say, give a longer piece time to develop, and return to an idea after living with it for a while.

That's part of what we want to make space for with Lettercamp: writing with a life beyond the day it was published.
